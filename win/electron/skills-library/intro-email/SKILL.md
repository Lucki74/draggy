# Intro email

## Goal

Connect two people so that both want to reply. A good introduction is short, shows why each gains from the contact, and carries the consent of both sides.

## When to use

- Introducing a job seeker to someone in a field, two founders, a client and a supplier.
- Responding to a request: "Could you introduce me to X?"
- Closing the loop after a meeting: "You two should talk."

For writing to a stranger without a go-between, use `cold-outreach`. For the thank-you afterward, use `thank-you-note`.

## Steps

1. **Collect the details.** Call `ask_user`: who are A and B, how the user knows each, what A wants, why B might help, and any limits (confidential topics, time, language). Ask for links to each person's public profile if they will help.
2. **Get consent first.** Ask whether B has agreed to be introduced. If not, write a short opt-in message to B instead: describe A in two lines and ask if B is open to a call. Draft the full introduction only after yes. This is the step most introducers skip.
3. **Write the opt-in message when needed.** Two or three sentences to B: who A is, what they want, how little time it takes, and an easy way to decline.
4. **Draft the double intro.**
   - Subject: "Intro: A and B" with the shared topic.
   - First line: the reason for the connection in one sentence.
   - One short paragraph on A, with the detail that matters to B. One short paragraph on B, with the detail that matters to A.
   - The suggested topic or ask, kept small.
   - The handoff: say who writes first, and release the user from the thread ("I'll let you take it from here").
5. **Check the balance.** Both paragraphs should be about equally warm and equally specific. If one side looks like the favor-giver, say plainly why the other side offers value too.
6. **Check privacy.** Include only what each person would be happy for the other to see. Leave out salary, health, private plans and confidential projects.
7. **Check facts.** Titles, company names and spellings come from the user or a visible profile. If unsure, mark the spot.
8. **Keep it short.** Under 150 words in total.
9. **Plan the aftermath.** Suggest the user check in lightly with each side after a couple of weeks only if it helps, not as a demand for a report.

## Gotchas

- Sending the introduction without B's agreement puts B in an awkward spot and burns the user's credit with them.
- Telling A one story and B another leaves them confused when they talk. Use the same reason in both paragraphs.
- Without a named first mover, both wait. State who writes.
- Over-praising ("the best engineer I know") raises expectations nobody can meet. Use concrete facts.
- Do not add unrelated people to copy. A wider thread makes it harder for either to decline.
- Forwarding A's private email as the intro exposes things A did not agree to share. Rewrite it.
- Introducing someone to a person who is hiring, then pressing them to act, puts B in a bind. Keep any pressure out of the message.
- An introduction between competitors or people with a conflict of interest needs a plain note about it.

## Output

If consent is missing: the opt-in message to B. If consent exists: the introduction email with subject. Add one line at the end saying what was assumed. No tips.
