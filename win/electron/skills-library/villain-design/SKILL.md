# Villain design

## Goal

Create an opponent who is the hero of their own story. A good antagonist wants something the reader can understand, uses means that threaten the protagonist's weak spot and forces choices that reveal the hero.

## When to use

- The plot lacks a credible opposing force.
- The existing villain is a cardboard menace or a speech-maker.
- The user needs a rival, a corrupt institution or a non-human antagonist.

For the hero, use `character-profile`. For the setting around them, use `worldbuilding`. For a power system they use, use `magic-system-design`.

## Steps

1. **Read the story facts.** Call `read_file` for notes or ask with `ask_user` for: the protagonist's want and flaw, the genre, the central conflict and the ending in mind. The villain is built against the hero.
2. **Choose the type.** A person, a group, a system, nature, or the hero's own side. Ask which fits, and say that an institution works as an antagonist if it has human faces.
3. **Give a goal that makes sense.** One concrete aim, such as "keep the dam from opening upstream", rooted in a need: security, love, justice, status, fear. The aim must collide with the hero's.
4. **Write the case.** In two sentences, state the villain's argument for why they are right. A reader should be able to half agree.
5. **Pick the line they will cross.** Name what they will do that the hero will not. This is what makes them dangerous and the story tense.
6. **Match the villain to the hero's weakness.** Design pressure on the flaw: if the hero avoids conflict, the villain forces it; if the hero trusts too easily, the villain is warm.
7. **Define the methods.** List three ways the villain acts on the world, from small to large, so the threat escalates. Include resources and limits: what they cannot do.
8. **Give them a human detail.** A habit, a loyalty, a fear. One small contradiction, such as kindness to one person, keeps them from a type.
9. **Plan their scenes.** Decide where the villain appears before the climax, what the reader learns each time and how each meeting raises cost for the hero.
10. **Plan the end.** Decide how the villain falls or wins, and what it says about the theme. Check that the hero's choice, not luck, decides it.
11. **Write the villain's best line.** One sentence from their argument that would make a reader nod. It confirms the case holds.
12. **Check the hero's side.** Ask what the villain takes from the hero and whether the hero ever agrees with them.

## Gotchas

- Evil with no reason is a plot device. Give a motive that the villain would defend in an argument.
- A villain who explains their plan in a long speech wastes tension. Show the plan through acts.
- Making the villain a copy of the hero in a darker coat is common. Make the difference concrete: methods, values or limits.
- Sympathy is not excuse. A reader can understand the villain and still want them stopped.
- Do not base a villain's traits on real illness, disability or a real group as shorthand for menace. Choose behavior, not identity.
- A villain who only appears in the last act has no weight. Seed them early, even by effect.
- Villains who lose every fight before the end look weak. Let them win at least once.
- A last-minute redemption without setup feels like a cheat. Plant it early or drop it.

## Output

A profile with headings: Type, Goal and need, The case, The line they cross, Pressure on the hero, Methods (three), Human detail, Scene plan, Ending. About 350 to 500 words. Offer to write a first scene with them.

If the story has several antagonists, rank them by threat so scenes do not repeat the same pressure.
