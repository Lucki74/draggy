# Money habits check

## Goal

Help the user notice how they handle money day to day and pick one or two small changes they believe in. The skill listens first and avoids judgment. It gives no investment advice and names no products; for decisions about debt or investing, a licensed advisor is the right person.

## When to use

- The user feels money slips away but cannot say why.
- The user wants to start with habits before building a full budget.
- Numbers-heavy review of a month is `use_skill` with `monthly-budget-review`.

## Steps

1. Ask with `ask_user` what prompted the question and what the user wants money to do for them in a year. Keep the question open.
2. Ask five short questions, one at a time: how often they look at their balance, how they decide on non-essential purchases, what happens in the week before payday, how they handle bills, and what they would do if an unexpected cost of a month's rent arrived.
3. Reflect back what you heard in two or three sentences using their words, and ask if it fits.
4. Name the strengths first. Find at least two real ones in the answers.
5. Find one or two friction points. Phrase them as patterns, not faults, for example money feels tight before payday.
6. For each friction point offer two or three small experiments the user can try for four weeks, such as a weekly five-minute balance check, a wait period before certain purchases, or paying bills on one day.
7. Let the user choose. If they pick more than two, ask which one matters most.
8. Turn the choice into a measurable habit: what, when, how often, and how they will know it happened.
9. Set a check-in date and a plain question to ask then: did it happen, what got in the way, keep, change or drop.
10. Ask what they would like to keep exactly as it is, so the changes protect what works.
11. Offer a short note the user can read at the next check-in.

## Gotchas

- Shaming the user for past choices, which ends the conversation.
- Setting many changes at once. One habit done is better than five planned.
- Giving fixed rules such as a percentage split as if universal. Ask about the user's priorities.
- Mixing the habit with the outcome. Track the action, such as the weekly check, not the balance.
- Assuming the user's situation matches yours. Incomes, dependants and debts differ widely.
- Diving into numbers before the person has said what matters to them.
- Turning curiosity into a lecture. Ask more than you tell.
- Choosing the habit for the user because it worked for someone else.

## Output

A short reflection, the strengths, one or two experiments with a measure and the check-in date. Keep it warm, brief and free of promises about results.

Close with one question: what is the smallest first step?
