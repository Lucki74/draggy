# Story writer

## Goal

Write fiction with a character who wants something, an obstacle, a turn and an ending that lands, in the genre, length and voice the user asked for.

## When to use

- The user asks for a short story, a scene, a bedtime story or fan fiction.
- The user wants help developing or continuing creative writing.
- For a blog article or factual essay, use `use_skill` with `blog-post`.

## Steps

1. Read the brief: genre, length, audience (children, adults), point of view, tone, characters, setting, and any required events or ending. Fill gaps with choices that suit the request. Ask at most one question before writing, unless the request is very open.
2. Set the want and obstacle. The main character wants something concrete, and something stands in the way. That is the story.
3. Start late. Open in the middle of action or tension, not with the weather or a wake-up.
4. Show through detail. Specific sensory details and actions reveal feelings better than naming them.
5. Write dialogue in short, distinct voices, each line either revealing character or moving the plot. Do not let characters explain what both already know.
6. Place a turn near the end: something changes, a choice is made, or a truth is revealed.
7. Resolve the want (achieved, lost, or transformed) and land on an image or line that echoes the start.
8. Pace deliberately. Slow down for important moments and summarise the rest.
9. Match the audience:
   - Children: simple sentences, repetition and rhythm, gentle stakes, warmth, and a comforting end for bedtime stories. Nothing frightening for young children.
   - Adults: trust the reader and leave some things unsaid.
   - Fan fiction: stay true to the established characters' voices.

## Gotchas

- Stories that open with background, weather or waking up lose the reader before anything happens.
- Naming a feeling ("she was sad") is weaker than showing a gesture or detail.
- Dialogue where characters recite shared knowledge for the reader's benefit reads as exposition.
- A story with no real obstacle has no arc, however good the sentences are.
- Content must fit the stated audience, especially for children.

## Output

A title, then the story. For longer pieces, use scene breaks (a blank line and a centred *). After the story, offer in one line a possible direction for revision or continuation.
