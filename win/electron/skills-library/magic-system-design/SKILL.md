# Magic system design

## Goal

Make a power system the reader can understand well enough to be surprised by it. A good system has a source, a cost, firm limits and effects on society, so the characters still have to struggle, plan and choose.

## When to use

- The user is writing fantasy or science fantasy, or designing a game's powers.
- Magic in a draft solves everything or feels arbitrary.
- The user needs rules for a tabletop campaign.

For the wider setting, use `worldbuilding`. For a villain using the system, use `villain-design`. For a playable scenario, use `tabletop-adventure`.

## Steps

1. **Read the notes.** Call `read_file` if the world exists. Ask with `ask_user` for: the story's central conflict, whether magic is rare or common, hard-rules or mysterious tone, and how it is meant to feel (scientific, spiritual, dangerous, playful).
2. **Decide how much the reader must know.** Hard systems with explicit rules let magic solve plot problems. Soft systems create wonder and should not solve problems. Pick a point on that scale and keep to it.
3. **Define the source.** Where power comes from: a place, a body, a bargain, a resource, a language. One source is enough to start.
4. **Define the method.** How a person uses it: words, gestures, substances, tools, training, a gift at birth. State who can learn it and how long it takes.
5. **Set the cost.** Every use costs something: energy, memory, years, a resource, a favor owed, a risk to others. The cost should hurt in ways the plot can use.
6. **Set the limits.** At least three firm things magic cannot do, and why. Limits are more useful than powers.
7. **Describe the social effects.** Who has access, who controls it, how it changes work, war, law, medicine, religion, class. If magic can heal, ask what that does to a society.
8. **Test with the plot.** Run three scenes through the rules: the hero's first use, a use that goes wrong, a use that could end the story too early. Adjust the rules until the third scene needs another solution.
9. **Add flavor.** Give magic a look, sound and feel, plus a local word or two. It should have texture beyond rules.
10. **Write the one-page summary.** A rules list the writer can check while drafting, plus open questions to decide later.
11. **Write the failure modes.** List how a use of magic goes wrong: miscasting, overuse, interception. These make good set pieces.
12. **Write one rule as a headline.** Summarize the system in one sentence a reader could repeat. If you cannot, simplify.

## Gotchas

- Power with no cost removes tension. Even a small price creates choices.
- Limits that appear only when the plot needs them break trust. Establish them early and stick to them.
- If a spell can do anything, ask why the characters do not use it for everything. The system must account for that.
- Magic named after the most obvious thing (fire, ice) offers little surprise. Look for unusual applications within the same rules.
- Copying a famous system with renamed parts is easy to spot. Change the source, cost or social effect.
- A system too large for the book needs a long exposition. Keep to what the story uses.
- A long list of schools, orders and ranks looks rich but hides the core idea. Start with one clear mechanism.
- If magic and technology share a world, say how they interact, or readers will ask.

## Output

A rules sheet with Source, Method, Cost, Limits (three or more), Who can use it, Social effects, Look and feel, Three test scenes with results, Open questions. About 400 to 650 words.

If the system is for a game, add a note on balance: what stops a player from using it in every scene.
